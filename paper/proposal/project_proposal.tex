\begin{abstract}
This proposal targets the course final project requirement for a substantial Python-based machine learning system. The project studies whether data assimilation (DA) can improve sequential video prediction when only sparse observations are available after the observed prefix. The central idea is that long-horizon video prediction is an unstable extrapolation problem: model errors accumulate unless the prediction is repeatedly re-anchored to reality. We propose to formulate video rollout as a latent-space forecast--analysis process, train a lightweight open-loop predictor as the DA host model, and apply latent ensemble Kalman updates whenever sparse observations become available. The initial study will focus on Moving MNIST with simulated noisy observations derived from held-out future frames. The project will evaluate both prediction quality and DA-specific uncertainty metrics, including RMSE, PCC, entropy, relative entropy, and mutual information. The main expected outcome is a clear demonstration of how assimilation changes the accuracy and uncertainty of sequential video prediction, together with a principled discussion of when DA helps and when it becomes computationally expensive.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

Sequential prediction problems become increasingly unstable as the rollout horizon grows. In open-loop video prediction, each forecasted frame becomes the input for the next step, so local prediction errors compound over time. This makes long-horizon prediction qualitatively different from interpolation or short-range forecasting: the farther the prediction extends beyond the observed prefix, the less constrained it is by reality.

Data assimilation offers a natural response to this instability. Rather than extrapolating indefinitely, DA periodically corrects the forecast whenever new observations arrive. From this perspective, stable prediction of a known world should not rely on unconstrained extrapolation alone; it should instead combine model-based forecasting with repeated re-anchoring to sparse evidence. This proposal asks whether that philosophy can be made concrete for video prediction in a small, controlled machine-learning project.

\section{Problem Statement}

Let $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ be the video frame at time $t$ and $z_t$ be its latent representation. The project will study sparse-conditioned video prediction in which:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item the first $T_c$ frames are observed as context,
    \item the next $T_p$ frames must be predicted sequentially,
    \item sparse observations of future frames may arrive intermittently during rollout.
\end{enumerate}

The latent forecast model is written as
\begin{equation}
    z_{t+1}^{f} = M_{\theta}(z_t^{a}) + \eta_{t+1},
\end{equation}
where $M_{\theta}$ is a learned latent dynamics model and $\eta_{t+1}$ is forecast uncertainty. Observations are modeled as
\begin{equation}
    y_{t+1} = H(x_{t+1}) + \epsilon_{t+1},
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the observation operator and $\epsilon_{t+1}$ is observation noise. The analysis step updates the latent forecast through
\begin{equation}
    z_{t+1}^{a} = z_{t+1}^{f} + K_{t+1}\left(y_{t+1} - H(D(z_{t+1}^{f}))\right),
\end{equation}
with Kalman gain estimated from a latent ensemble.

The core research question is:
\begin{quote}
Can latent-space data assimilation improve the quality and uncertainty behavior of sparse-conditioned video prediction compared with open-loop rollout of the same predictor?
\end{quote}

\section{Proposed Method}

\subsection{Reference Baseline and DA Host}

The project will use two model roles:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item a literature-aware reference baseline such as SimVP \citep{gao2022simvp},
    \item a lightweight open-loop latent predictor that serves as the DA host model.
\end{enumerate}

The DA host model will consist of:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item a convolutional encoder,
    \item a ConvLSTM latent dynamics module,
    \item a residual latent prediction head,
    \item a convolutional decoder.
\end{itemize}

Given context frames $x_{1:T_c}$, the model encodes them into latent feature maps and builds a recurrent state. It then predicts future latent states autoregressively:
\begin{align}
    h_{t+1}, c_{t+1} &= \mathrm{ConvLSTM}(z_t, h_t, c_t), \\
    \hat{z}_{t+1} &= z_t + G(h_{t+1}), \\
    \hat{x}_{t+1} &= D(\hat{z}_{t+1}).
\end{align}

\subsection{Observation Design}

The most important modeling decision is the observation source. In real-world long-horizon video generation, there may be no obvious future observation stream. To study the DA mechanism in a controlled and defensible way, the initial project will use \emph{simulated observations} generated from held-out ground truth at test time. This is standard in DA benchmarking and is not considered cheating as long as:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item the future truth is not used during training,
    \item only partial/noisy observations are revealed,
    \item the model must still infer the unobserved future structure.
\end{enumerate}

The initial observation regimes will include:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item full-frame observations every $k$ future steps,
    \item masked-pixel observations,
    \item low-resolution frame observations,
    \item additive Gaussian observation noise.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Latent ETKF}

At each assimilation step, an ensemble of latent forecast states will be propagated through the host model. The predicted frames will be projected into observation space and compared with the available observation. An ETKF-style update will then correct the latent ensemble before the rollout continues.

This is attractive for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item latent space is much lower-dimensional than pixel space,
    \item ensemble updates provide a natural uncertainty estimate,
    \item the forecast--analysis structure matches the DA interpretation exactly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Plan}

\subsection{Dataset}

The primary benchmark will be Moving MNIST, using:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item grayscale frames of size $64 \times 64$,
    \item two moving digits per sequence,
    \item $20$ frames per sequence,
    \item prediction task: first $10$ frames $\rightarrow$ next $10$ frames.
\end{itemize}

This dataset is intentionally simple. The goal is not to claim realism, but to make the DA mechanism transparent and fast enough to implement, debug, and analyze within the project timeline.

\subsection{Baselines}

The main comparisons will be:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item SimVP reference baseline,
    \item open-loop DA host without any future assimilation,
    \item DA host with always-assimilate latent ETKF,
    \item optional selective-assimilation extension if time permits.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The project will report the required DA metrics:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item RMSE,
    \item PCC,
    \item entropy,
    \item relative entropy,
    \item mutual information.
\end{enumerate}

Their purpose is different:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item RMSE and PCC measure prediction quality,
    \item entropy measures forecast uncertainty,
    \item relative entropy measures how strongly the observation changes the forecast,
    \item mutual information measures how much uncertainty the observation removes.
\end{itemize}

Runtime and number of assimilation events will also be recorded, because a longer-term motivation of the project is to understand the quality--compute tradeoff.

\section{Expected Outcomes}

The main expected outcome is that latent ETKF will improve prediction quality relative to the same open-loop host model under sparse observations. More specifically, the project expects to show:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item lower RMSE and higher PCC after assimilation,
    \item lower posterior uncertainty compared with the open-loop forecast,
    \item interpretable entropy / relative entropy / mutual information values that reflect a genuine forecast--analysis correction process.
\end{enumerate}

The project does \emph{not} assume that always-assimilate ETKF will immediately be faster than open-loop prediction. In fact, a likely outcome is that ETKF improves quality but costs more runtime. This would still be a useful finding, because it would motivate a later selective-assimilation extension in which corrections are applied only when innovation, spread, or entropy is sufficiently large.

By the end of the project, the intended deliverables are:

\section{Risks and Limitations}

The project has several clear risks:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item the open-loop host may be too weak or too blurry,
    \item latent ETKF may improve quality only slightly,
    \item the observation setup may be criticized if it is not framed carefully,
    \item the method may improve quality without improving compute.
\end{enumerate}

These risks will be addressed by:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item keeping the benchmark controlled and transparent,
    \item explicitly treating the observation stream as a synthetic DA benchmark,
    \item comparing DA against the same open-loop host rather than changing multiple things at once,
    \item discussing quality improvement and runtime cost separately.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This proposal frames latent-space data assimilation as a machine-learning project about stabilizing sequential prediction with structured probabilistic updates. The project is deliberately scoped to a clean benchmark so that the key ideas remain analyzable: a learned latent predictor, sparse observations, ETKF correction, and uncertainty-aware metrics. If successful, the project will provide a concrete demonstration that DA can improve sparse-conditioned video prediction and offer a principled way to think about long-horizon forecasting as repeated local re-anchoring rather than unconstrained extrapolation.
